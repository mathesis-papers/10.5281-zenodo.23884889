Proof of Mathar's conjecture on OEIS A027182: a closed form and the six-term recurrence

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Some integer sequences are built by combining every entry in a row of a number triangle, yet appear to obey a shorter rule connecting neighboring terms. Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang give an explicit formula explaining one such pattern and use it to prove a recurrence conjectured by R. J. Mathar, together with four related conjectures.

The triangle, introduced by Clark Kimberling and catalogued as OEIS A027170, is constructed from Pascal’s triangle. A binomial coefficient counts ways of choosing a specified number of objects from a larger collection. The authors simplify the entry in position k of row n to twice the number of ways to choose k+1 objects from n+2, plus the number of ways to choose k from n, minus four. Positions run from zero to n. Regard these entries as polynomial coefficients, in increasing powers of a placeholder x. Squaring this polynomial and taking the coefficient of its middle power defines A027182. This calculation involves increasingly many products; a recurrence would replace it with a fixed-length rule.

For example, row two is (5, 10, 5), giving the polynomial 5+10x+5x². Its square has middle coefficient 5·5+10·10+5·5=150. The key is that every row reads identically backward: the middle coefficient is therefore the sum of the squares of its entries. To express the answer, let cₘ count ways of choosing m objects from 2m objects; these are the central binomial coefficients. The authors establish aₙ=4cₙ₊₂+4cₙ₊₁+cₙ−72·2ⁿ+16n+40 for every n≥0. The original sequence starts at n=1; the same construction supplies a₀=1.

Why does this formula emerge? Write each entry as twice one binomial coefficient, plus another, minus four, and expand its square. Apart from the constant contribution, only five kinds of sums remain: two sums of squares, one sum of products, and two ordinary sums. Multiplying two binomial expansions counts the same choices as a single expansion with their sizes added. This evaluates the squared and mixed sums as central binomial coefficients; the ordinary sums give powers of two. Removing excluded endpoints where necessary produces the remaining corrections. Thus the authors directly evaluate the convolution, rather than fitting its first terms. They contrast this approach with general algorithms for binomial sums developed by Bostan, Lairez and Salvy.

The recurrence requires another step: factorials give a simple relation between consecutive central binomial coefficients. The authors use it to derive a second-order relation, with index-dependent coefficients, for the three-term combination in their formula. Applying suitable differences to this relation eliminates the exponential contribution and leaves a known linear remainder from the polynomial contribution. Shifting must move the index-dependent coefficients as well as sequence entries. The result is Mathar’s conjecture of 6 May 2026: an inhomogeneous relation with linear polynomial coefficients involving the current term and five preceding terms, valid for every n≥6. With the added value a₀, it also holds at n=5.

The four companion results concern the same triangle. The central entry of row 2n, A027171, satisfies its conjectured three-term relation for n≥2; the left central entry of row 2n−1, A027175, does so for n≥3. The entry two positions left of the center of row 2n, A027174, satisfies its relation for n≥4. These deductions use closed forms credited to Christian Krause in May 2026, not claimed here as new. Summing positions zero through the middle gives A027179 and its seven-term relation for n≥6. Symmetry makes this half the full row sum in odd rows; even rows need an extra half of the middle entry. Separating the parities reduces the proof to central binomial ratios and polynomial identities. The authors credit Mathar’s parity formulas and the row-sum formula recorded by Ralf Stephan in 2004 and proved by Krause in 2026.

The manuscript supplies written arguments for all stated ranges. Separately, the authors report Python comparisons through index 400 for the convolution formula and all five recurrences, plus exact checks of polynomial identities; those finite sequence checks cannot establish the infinite claims by themselves. They identify a pinned Lean theorem stating the main recurrence for n≥6, but do not claim corresponding Lean coverage for the four companions. The significance is a specific explanation and settlement of these five recorded conjectures through a common binomial structure, not a general classification of such triangles or recurrences. No further open conjecture is formulated in the note.
